## Shared exact-edition reading

Brazil's 1970 structure cannot be reduced to one rigid formation. In possession, converted midfielder Piazza gave the back line a first passer. Carlos Alberto supplied aggressive width on the right, while the less attacking Everaldo narrowed beside the centre-backs to preserve a back three. Clodoaldo protected the centre beneath the roaming attackers, and Gérson conducted play with short combinations and longer distribution.

Ahead of them, Rivellino began from the left but was a playmaker rather than a conventional winger. Jairzinho began on the right, helped the midfield without the ball, then surged into central scoring positions. Tostão remained the centre-forward but organised attacks by dropping to combine. Pelé also moved toward the ball, drawing defenders before choosing whether to release a runner or arrive as the scorer.

## Card record

### Brito — PROVE / PORTRAIT

Before:

> Brito stands out for using contact without losing his defensive position. One cue is how he stays goal-side until support is close enough for him to engage. Another is how he intercepts from the front once the pass exposes the receiver.

Final:

> Brito is the pure defender beside Piazza, tall and strong in the air while the converted midfielder handles more of the first pass. He rarely crosses halfway; within Brazil’s four-man line, his job is to delay the attack until an interception or tackle is available.

Evidence: L'Équipe describes Brito as the pure, tall, aerially strong defender beside the more technical first passer Piazza, and notes how rarely Brito crossed halfway. FIFA's contemporary report describes Brazil's four-man line delaying attacks until an interception or tackle became available.

Why better: the final replaces three precise but weakly sourced personal mechanics with Brito's documented partnership, aerial strength and positional restraint.

Limit: the delay-before-intervention method is documented for the entire back four, not uniquely for Brito. The wording preserves that boundary by locating the action within his line.

### Carlos Alberto — TWEAK / PORTRAIT

Before:

> For Alberto, start with choosing the overlap only after the space behind him is protected. Watch how he starts the forward run after a teammate can cover the space he leaves. Separately, he scans the far post before the opposite winger receives the ball.

Final:

> Carlos Alberto chooses the overlap only after Brazil can protect the space behind him. In the final, Jairzinho moves left and Facchetti follows; Pelé then releases the captain’s run into the open right flank for the fourth goal.

Evidence: FIFA and L'Équipe reconstruct Jairzinho moving left, Facchetti following him, Pelé receiving and Carlos Alberto entering the vacated right side. L'Équipe and Coaches' Voice also document the asymmetric full-back cover behind his advances.

Why better: it preserves the strong overlap-timing identity and replaces an unsupported far-post scan with the exact movement that best demonstrates it.

Limit: the example comes from one famous match. The opening sentence retains the broader role so the card does not reduce Carlos Alberto to the final goal.

### Clodoaldo — TWEAK / PORTRAIT

Before:

> What separates Clodoaldo is moving the ball forward without forcing the crowded route. Clodoaldo carries through the open lane if no forward pass is available. Separately, he recycles possession rather than isolating a teammate with a forced forward pass.

Final:

> Clodoaldo gives Brazil balance beneath their roaming attackers, protecting the centre and carrying forward when space opens. He arrives in the box to equalise against Uruguay, then escapes a crowd in midfield at the start of Carlos Alberto’s goal in the final.

Evidence: Coaches' Voice places Clodoaldo as the structural midfielder beneath the interchanging attackers, while Tostão's account supports his deeper position. FIFA records his semi-final equaliser and crowded midfield escape during the final's fourth-goal sequence.

Why better: the final explains why his carrying mattered inside this particular side and demonstrates it with two different exact-edition actions. The old safe-recycling sentence was plausible but interchangeable.

Limit: sources describe either four or five opponents beaten in the final sequence. The card says “a crowd” rather than choosing a disputed count.

### Everaldo — KEEP / PORTRAIT, then team-level correction

Before:

> Covering the outside lane without losing the route back to goal defines the way Everaldo plays. Look for how he drops back into the defensive line once possession is lost. The same quality appears when he turns toward his own goal early enough to match the first recovery run.

Final:

> Everaldo is Brazil’s less attacking full-back, narrowing beside Brito and Piazza when Carlos Alberto advances on the opposite side. That asymmetry leaves a back three behind the attack while the right-back joins forward.

Evidence: L'Équipe says Everaldo compensated for Carlos Alberto's advances and regularly narrowed beside Brito and Piazza. Coaches' Voice identifies him as the less attacking full-back in the covering back three.

Why better: the old card's broad defensive identity was plausible, but neither the post-loss drop nor the early recovery turn was directly established. The final names the exact relationship that made his 1970 role distinctive.

Limit: “less attacking” is comparative, not absolute. Everaldo could still advance; the card does not claim that he stayed permanently in defence.

### Félix — TWEAK / ROLE_GUIDE

Before:

> To follow Félix, begin with protecting the centre of goal before reacting to the final touch. He protects the central lane and forces the attacker toward the harder finish. Beyond that, he reads the striker's body shape before moving toward either post.

Final:

> Félix is uneasy when England send high crosses into the goalmouth. In the semi-final, he responds with a point-blank save from Cubilla when Uruguay break through. His tournament reads as that contrast: some aerial uncertainty, but also one decisive reaction save.

Evidence: FIFA's contemporary report says England's high deliveries caused difficulty for Félix and the defenders, calls him insecure on occasions, and separately records his point-blank save from Cubilla.

Why better: the generic ideal-goalkeeper shell is replaced with an honest exact-edition contrast between a documented weakness and an important save.

Limit: two match observations cannot support a complete goalkeeping portrait. This is intentionally a short role guide; “responds” links the tournament moments without claiming that he permanently solved the weakness.

### Gérson — KEEP / PORTRAIT, then researched replacement

Before:

> For Gérson, the starting point is seeing the next action before receiving under pressure. Notice how he draws pressure toward the ball and releases the runner beyond it. Beyond that, he slows the touch to give teammates time to move ahead of the ball.

Final:

> Gérson conducts Brazil from central midfield, joining short combinations and then changing the attack with a longer pass. His influence in the final is just as broad: he scores from the edge of the box, then delivers the free-kick Pelé heads across for Jairzinho.

Evidence: Coaches' Voice and L'Équipe identify Gérson as the deep conductor whose short combinations and longer left-footed distribution changed the attack. FIFA records his final goal and the free-kick Pelé headed across to Jairzinho.

Why better: the old opening was good, but its pressure-drawing and slowed-touch mechanics were less central than the passing range that made Gérson this team's conductor.

Limit: the second sentence uses the final heavily. It follows rather than replaces the broader midfield identity.

### Jairzinho — EDITION_SPLIT / PORTRAIT

Before:

> What separates Jairzinho is using the touchline to stretch the defence before moving inside. Jairzinho threatens outside before cutting behind midfield. Separately, he moves toward the far post as the attack develops on the opposite side.

Final:

> Jairzinho starts on the right and tracks back to help Clodoaldo, but his defining movement is the surge from the flank into central scoring positions. He scores in all six matches; against Uruguay he wins the ball near Brazil’s box and finishes the 73-metre counter 11 seconds later.

Evidence: Tostão describes Jairzinho's right-side defensive work and reconstructs the Uruguay recovery and counter. FIFA records the 73 metres, 11 seconds and scoring in all six matches. Coaches' Voice and L'Équipe support his movement from the right into central scoring positions.

Why better: it separates the 1970 scorer from his near-duplicate 1974 winger card. His defensive work, central runs and extraordinary tournament output now belong to one edition-specific identity.

Limit: “73-metre counter” compresses FIFA's measurement that possession was won 73 metres from goal. It does not imply that every touch in the move belonged to Jairzinho.

### Pelé — RESEARCH / PORTRAIT

Before:

> Watch Pelé for moving defenders before choosing the final action. First, notice how he draws defenders toward him before releasing the teammate beyond them. Separately, he waits for the defender to look away before accelerating.

Final:

> Watch Pelé for moving defenders before choosing the final action. He drops toward the ball to release runners but still arrives in the box as a scorer; in the final he heads the opener, then supplies Jairzinho and Carlos Alberto for the last two goals.

Evidence: FIFA documents Pelé drawing two defenders before Jairzinho's winner against England and several defenders before releasing Carlos Alberto in the final. L'Équipe supports his dropping movement, while the final report records his header and the actions supplying Brazil's third and fourth goals.

Why better: the strongest old headline survives exactly. The generic look-away acceleration and overlap with Tostão are replaced with Pelé's 1970 creator-scorer balance.

Limit: retrospective assist conventions can vary. “Supplies” describes the visible actions without relying on a tournament assist total.

### Rivellino — KEEP / PORTRAIT, then researched replacement

Before:

> Rivellino's signature is starting from the left and narrowing enough to keep the midfield connected. Watch how he opens the far side with his passing before pressure can close it. The same quality appears when he sets the ball outside the crowd so his shot can travel through the gap.

Final:

> Rivellino's signature is starting from the left and narrowing enough to keep the midfield connected. His left foot adds direct threat: his three goals come from around the edge of the box or farther out, and his cross creates Pelé's opener in the final.

Evidence: Tostão places Rivellino on the left of Brazil's diagonal midfield and L'Équipe identifies him as a playmaker rather than a fixed winger. FIFA locates two 1970 goals outside the box and the third only inches inside it; the final report records his cross for Pelé.

Why better: the strong opening remains. The second half now explains what distinguished Rivellino's left foot in this edition instead of relying on generic far-side and shooting-gap mechanics.

Limit: “around the edge” is deliberately approximate and avoids pretending that modern shot coordinates exist for every historical action.

### Tostão — TWEAK / PORTRAIT

Before:

> The key to Tostão is organising the attack by moving away from a fixed centre-forward position. He draws defenders toward him before releasing the teammate beyond them. Separately, he waits for the defender to look away before accelerating.

Final:

> Tostão is Brazil’s centre-forward, but his task is to organise rather than wait in the box. He drops to combine and releases runners: his dribble starts the winner against England, and his pass sends Jairzinho away from inside Brazil’s half against Uruguay.

Evidence: Tostão describes himself as the side's central, advanced organising forward rather than a traditional penalty-box striker. FIFA records his dribble and combination in the England winner; his own account supports the pass releasing Jairzinho against Uruguay.

Why better: it respects Tostão's own distinction instead of casually labelling him a false nine, and it separates his facilitation from Pelé's nearby portrait.

Limit: the examples emphasize creation rather than his two goals against Peru because the organising role is the more important football identity.

### Wilson Piazza — PROVE / ROLE_GUIDE

Before:

> To assess Piazza, start with stepping beyond the first pressure to change the point of attack. He opens his body after the recovery so the first pass can escape pressure. A different clue is how he holds the dangerous lane until a teammate can pressure the ball.

Final:

> Moved from midfield to centre-back, Piazza gives Brazil a defender who can start the next attack instead of only clearing danger. His passing connects the build-up through Clodoaldo and the full-backs, providing the base from which Brazil’s attackers interchange.

Evidence: Piazza's interview confirms Zagallo's exact positional conversion. Coaches' Voice describes the build-up beginning through Piazza toward Clodoaldo or the full-backs, and L'Équipe calls him the technical first passer beside Brito.

Why better: unsupported body-opening and lane-holding mechanics are replaced with the exact reason his conversion mattered to the team.

Limit: the evidence establishes a tactical role more confidently than repeated individual micro-actions. The final therefore stays concise rather than inventing more technique.

## Blind comparison and correction pass

The initial researched set comprised Carlos Alberto, Clodoaldo, Félix, Gérson, Jairzinho, Pelé, Rivellino, Tostão and Wilson Piazza.

Two independent reviewers received randomized old-versus-proposed pairs with exact-edition evidence constraints and no old/new labels. Both preferred all nine proposed portraits. After narrow wording concerns were repaired, two fresh reviewers compared old versus final with the orientations reversed. Both again selected all nine finals, with no ties and no old versions preferred.

The team-level self-critique then challenged the two controls separately:

- Brito's old contact, support-distance and front-interception mechanics were less directly evidenced than his aerial identity and partnership with Piazza. Two fresh reviewers, with reversed A/B orientation, both preferred the new card.
- Everaldo's old post-loss and early-turn mechanics were less directly evidenced than his asymmetric cover beside the centre-backs. Two fresh reviewers, again with reversed orientation, both preferred the narrow correction.

Rejected material included Carlos Alberto's far-post scan, Clodoaldo's safe-recycling sentence, Félix's centre-first and body-reading goalkeeper shell, Gérson's slowed-touch detail, Jairzinho's near-duplicate 1974 portrait, Pelé and Tostão's shared look-away acceleration, Rivellino's generic shooting-gap mechanics, and Piazza's body-opening and lane-holding claims.

## Team-level self-critique

The final team is substantially more distinct. Brito and Piazza read as complementary centre-backs; Carlos Alberto and Everaldo have deliberately asymmetric full-back jobs; Clodoaldo and Gérson have separate structural and creative functions; and the four attackers no longer sound like rearrangements of the same template.

Five cards mention the final. That repetition is visible, but each event demonstrates a different identity: Carlos Alberto's overlap, Clodoaldo's carrying, Gérson's passing range, Pelé's scorer-creator balance and Rivellino's delivery. Removing those references solely for cadence would weaken the football explanation.

Félix is the least complete portrait and the only card with fewer than three machine-detectable content beats. That is an honest consequence of the evidence ceiling, not an invitation to add filler.

Changing all eleven cards is a reason for scrutiny. Three began as `KEEP`, and Phase B must not become a ritual that defeats the classification pass. Here, however, the final reviews identified a specific weakness in each old card, each replacement had exact-edition support, and every comparison preferred the final. Brazil 1970 also has unusually rich footage and source material. This outcome should not become an expected rewrite rate for later teams.

## Locale review

Only the eleven approved English changes were translated into Spanish, Korean and Chinese.

- Spanish uses a natural Latin American register, including *mediocampo*, *área chica*, *atajada a quemarropa*, *juego aéreo* and *mitad de la cancha*. Jairzinho's location was corrected from *área brasileña* to *área de Brasil*.
- Korean keeps the actions compact rather than reproducing English syntax mechanically. Existing newsroom forms were followed for `카를루스 아우베르투 토히스` and `호베르투 히벨리누`.
- Chinese names were aligned with nearby 1970 copy, including `雅伊尔津尼奥`, `克洛多阿尔多`, `布里托` and `皮亚扎`.

The translations retain the evidence boundaries: Brito's delay-and-intervene action remains a back-four responsibility; Everaldo is comparatively less attacking rather than static; Félix remains a concise mixed assessment; and Jairzinho's 73-metre and 11-second figures remain unchanged.

No material prose uncertainty remains. A separate pre-existing issue is visible in Chinese: some card headers use Latin or generated player names even when body copy uses the established Chinese form. Header localization was outside this sprint and was not changed.

## Final self-critique

Brazil 1970 is a clear net improvement and supports continuing to the next team-edition. All eleven final cards beat their predecessors in independent blind comparison; the two controls were changed only after separate evidence reviews identified narrow, repairable defects.

The side now reads as a connected football team without collapsing into one house cadence. The descriptions differ because the evidence differs: partnership for Brito and Piazza, spatial timing for the full-backs, honest contrast for Félix, passing range for Gérson, two-way scoring runs for Jairzinho, final-action choice for Pelé, left-footed threat for Rivellino and facilitation for Tostão.

The caution for the next team is not to copy Brazil's event density or its 100% change rate. The final is unusually rich evidence for this side, and the historical record is unusually strong. Future teams should still preserve good cards, let identity lead, and use incidents only when they genuinely demonstrate it.

No process wall requires approval before proceeding. No Best XI rationale, UI code, broad generator framework, unrelated card, commit, push or deployment was changed.
